Every cup of coffee starts as a fruit. It's easy to forget, because by the time it reaches you, almost nothing about it looks like it ever grew on a tree.

## On the branch

Coffee cherries ripen unevenly, even on a single branch, from green to yellow to a deep red. On the best farms, pickers come back to the same trees several times in a season and take only the ripe ones. It's slow, expensive work, and it's the first place where quality is won or lost.

Inside each cherry are usually two seeds, facing each other with their flat sides together. Those seeds are the coffee beans.

## Out of the fruit

The fruit has to come off, and how it comes off changes the flavour. Washed coffees are stripped of their fruit and fermented in water before drying, which gives them a clean, bright taste. Natural coffees are dried whole in the sun, with the fruit still on, and taste heavier and fruitier. Honey coffees sit between the two.

After drying, the seeds rest, are hulled and sorted, and are packed into sacks as green coffee. In that state they keep for months.

## Into the fire

A roaster turns the green seed into something you'd recognise. Inside the drum, sugars caramelise and hundreds of aroma compounds form. About ten minutes in, the beans crack audibly as steam escapes. Stopping soon after that first crack gives a light roast that still tastes of where it grew; going further trades that character for roasty, bittersweet flavours.

## Into the cup

From the moment it leaves the roaster, the coffee starts to change. For a few days it releases carbon dioxide, and for a few weeks after that it's at its best. Then you grind it, pour water over it, and in a few minutes dissolve about a fifth of it into your cup.

From a cherry on a hillside to a few minutes in a kettle's steam, the journey takes most of a year. It's worth drinking slowly.
