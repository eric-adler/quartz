Espresso is the most demanding way to make coffee. Hot water is forced through a fine, compacted bed of grounds in under thirty seconds, so every variable is magnified. Here is what happens inside the machine, the puck and the cup.

## The machine

### Pressure

A pump pushes water through the coffee at around 9 bar, about nine times the air pressure around you. That pressure is what lets water pass through grounds as fine as [[Grind size|table salt]] in seconds rather than minutes. Lever machines and modern pumps can vary the pressure through the shot: starting gently and ramping up reduces [[Channeling]].

### Temperature stability

Water should hit the coffee at a steady 90–96 °C. Cheap machines swing by several degrees between shots, which shows up as inconsistent taste. Better machines use heavy brass groups, heat exchangers or separate boilers to keep the [[Water temperature]] flat.

## The puck

### Dose

A double shot typically uses 16–20 g of coffee. The dose fixes everything downstream: more coffee means more resistance and more to extract. Pick a dose that fits the basket and keep it constant while you adjust other things.

### Distribution and tamping

The grounds must be spread evenly before they are compressed. Any clump or thin spot becomes a fast lane for water. Tamping pressure matters less than people think. What matters is that the tamp is level and the bed has no gaps. A good [[Burr grinder]] makes this much easier, because uniform grounds pack uniformly.

## The shot

### Ratio and time

A classic recipe: 18 g in, 36 g out, in 25–30 seconds. That is a [[Brew ratio]] of 1:2. Longer ratios (1:2.5, 1:3) give lighter, sweeter shots; shorter ones (1:1.5) give dense, syrupy ones. Time is a symptom, not a target: if the shot runs fast, grind finer; if slow, grind coarser.

### Taste the shot

Sour, thin and salty means under-extracted. Bitter, dry and hollow means over-extracted. A good shot is sweet, with [[Acidity]] that tastes like fruit rather than vinegar, and a thick, syrupy [[Body]]. The [[Crema]] on top tells you the beans are fresh, not that the shot is good.

## When it goes wrong

### Channeling

Water finds a crack and rushes through it. That path over-extracts while the rest under-extracts, so the shot is sour _and_ bitter. Look for spurts, a pale stream, or pinholes in the spent puck.

### Gushing and choking

If the shot gushes in ten seconds, the grind is too coarse or the dose too small. If it chokes and drips for a minute, the opposite. Change one thing at a time; see [[Extraction]] for why.

## Milk

### Microfoam

Most espresso is drunk with milk. Steaming adds air and heat; done well, it produces [[Milk and microfoam|microfoam]], glossy milk with bubbles too small to see. It folds into the espresso instead of floating on top, which is what makes latte art possible.

### Temperature

Milk tastes sweetest around 55–65 °C. Above that the proteins break down and the [[Sweetness]] disappears, which is why a scalding cappuccino tastes flat.
