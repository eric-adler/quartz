A good cup of coffee is less mysterious than the vocabulary around it suggests. Almost everything a barista adjusts is a way of controlling one process: [[Extraction]], the amount of coffee that ends up dissolved in your cup.

## The dials

The strongest dial is [[Grind size]]. Finer grounds expose more surface, so water works faster; coarser grounds slow everything down. [[Water temperature]] does the same job more gently, and contact time quietly finishes it.

Separate from all of that is strength, set by the [[Brew ratio]]: how much coffee you use for how much water. A cup can be strong and under-extracted, or weak and over-extracted. If you want to stop guessing, a refractometer measures [[Total dissolved solids]], and the two numbers come apart.

Underneath everything sits [[Water chemistry]]. A cup is about 98% water, and its minerals decide what dissolves and how the acidity reads.

## The beans

What you start with sets the ceiling. [[Arabica and robusta]] are the two species behind nearly all coffee, and they taste very different. Within arabica, [[Terroir]] (altitude, soil, climate, variety) gives each origin its character, and [[Processing methods]] can push that character towards clean and bright or towards heavy and fruity.

Then comes the roaster. [[Roast level]] decides how much of that origin survives, and from the moment roasting stops, [[Freshness]] starts counting down.

## The method

Different methods are different deals with the same physics. [[Espresso]] uses pressure and a fine grind to extract in under thirty seconds, and the foam on top, [[Crema]], is mostly a sign of fresh beans. [[Pour-over]] trades speed for clarity: a paper filter, a [[Bloom]] to let the gas escape, then a slow, steady pour.

## When it goes wrong

The most common complaint is [[Bitterness]]. It has three usual causes, and only one of them is about the beans you bought.
